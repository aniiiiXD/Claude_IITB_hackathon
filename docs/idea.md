# Nidaan — The Complete Idea

> *Nidaan* (निदान) — Sanskrit/Hindi for *diagnosis*, *clarity*, *the act of identifying the cause*.

---

## One sentence

**Nidaan is a multi-agent clinical decision support system that compresses the five-link chain between a child's first symptom and the medicine that saves them — from years to a single afternoon — by being the connective tissue between recognition, confirmation, funding, drug access, and treatment.**

---

## The Problem: The Five-Link Chain

A treatable rare disease in India is not primarily a medical problem. It is a logistics problem dressed up as a medical one. There are five sequential failures between a child's first symptom and the medicine that saves them. **Any single break in this chain is fatal.** India breaks all five by default.

The headline number — *70 million Indians live with some form of rare disease* — gets stuck in public conversation. But that number includes mild conditions. The numbers that actually decide who lives and who dies are smaller and sharper:

- **30%** of children with rare diseases die before age 5
- **7 years** average diagnostic odyssey in India (4.7 globally)
- **30%** NPRD funding application approval rate
- **₹2.93 lakh** raised on the official rare-disease crowdfunding portal since 2021 — annual need is **₹91 billion**
- **12** Centres of Excellence across **28 states + 8 union territories** — twenty states have none
- **5** labs in all of India can run enzyme assays for lysosomal storage disorders
- **125.6 km** average travel distance to a treatment infusion centre
- **>95%** of treatable rare-disease patients in India are excluded purely by drug price

### Link 1 — Recognition (the doctor never thinks of it)

A district-hospital GP sees one rare-disease patient a year. The textbook differential is malaria, TB, kala-azar, leukemia. The rare diagnosis is not on the list.

- **43%** of practising physicians in India have never seen a single rare-disease patient in their career *(ClinMed survey)*
- **31%** of researchers and **29%** of HCPs surveyed could not estimate the time required for diagnosis of a rare disease they had themselves listed familiarity with *(Baseline Knowledge of Rare Diseases in India, ClinMed 2023)*
- **~50** total genetic counsellors in a country of 1.4 billion

> **Real case — A 12-year-old girl, malaria-endemic India.** Splenomegaly, epistaxis, recurrent fevers. Her positive malaria test "explained" the swollen spleen; nobody ordered a bone-marrow biopsy or an enzyme assay. Gaucher disease was confirmed only years later, after bone marrow biopsy. *Source: PMC10807272 / Dove Press.*

**Where Nidaan plugs in:** A 6-agent specialist conference that fires inside a 10-minute consult, surfacing the rare differential at the *first* visit before the patient is lost to follow-up.

### Link 2 — Confirmation (the lab is too far, or doesn't exist)

Even when a doctor suspects rare disease, enzyme assays and gene sequencing live in five labs across the entire country — almost all in metros. The enzyme assay costs ₹2,000–4,000. It exists at AIIMS Delhi, KEM Mumbai, CMC Vellore, FRIGE Ahmedabad, CDFD Hyderabad. It only gets ordered if someone first thinks: *this is Gaucher.*

- **63%** of Wilson disease cases in India are misdiagnosed; mean delay 2 years, range up to **30 years** *(Prashanth et al., Indian Annals of Neurology)*
- Slit-lamps for detecting Kayser-Fleischer rings are not routinely available below tertiary level — yet KF rings are pathognomonic for Wilson's

> **Real case — A 12-year-old boy, rural Maharashtra.** Presented at age 10 with ascites, jaundice, and Kayser-Fleischer rings — pathognomonic for Wilson's disease. The rural facility had no slit-lamp. He was misdiagnosed. The Wilson's diagnosis was confirmed at autopsy, two years after first presentation. *Source: Indian Journal of Pathology and Oncology.*

**Where Nidaan plugs in:** Routes the GP to the nearest accredited lab with sample-collection instructions, costs in ₹, and a pre-filled requisition form. The doctor doesn't need to know which lab can run β-glucocerebrosidase — Nidaan does.

### Link 3 — Funding (NPRD exists, the application gets stuck)

NPRD 2021 promises ₹50 lakh per patient. The Centre of Excellence files the application. The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare approves three out of ten.

- **30%** NPRD application approval rate, per a senior Indian rare-disease physician quoted in *Global Health NOW* (Nov 2025)
- **₹50 lakh** lifetime cap is insufficient — eliglustat for Gaucher costs **₹1.8 crore/year**
- The official government crowdfunding portal at `rarediseases.nhp.gov.in` has raised **₹2.93 lakh total** since launch in 2021. **3,981** patients are registered. Annual need across them: **~₹91 billion** *(ORF, ORD India)*

> **Real case — Arohi Kajabe, rural Maharashtra.** Gaucher disease type 1. Two ERT injections a month — at $1,200 each — would have stopped the progression. Her father Yogesh, a daily-wage cotton farmer, sold his land and borrowed over $6,000. The Ministry never approved the hospital's NPRD application. Arohi died.
>
> *"My only child died before my eyes because I couldn't afford the medicines."* — Yogesh Kajabe
>
> *Source: Global Health NOW, November 2025.*

**Where Nidaan plugs in:** Pre-fills the NPRD application from case data the moment a CoE specialist confirms the diagnosis. The gap between *eligible* and *applied* — currently the largest single source of preventable rare-disease deaths in India — closes to zero.

### Link 4 — Drug access (the medicine costs more than the cap)

₹50 lakh sounds like a lot until the drug is ₹1.8 crore a year. The structural absurdity:

- Indian manufacturers **export the active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs)** for orphan drugs to foreign companies
- Those foreign companies finish the drugs and **sell them back to India** at imported-drug prices
- **No domestic orphan drug manufacturing pathway** under CDSCO, even though India is the global hub for generics
- Result: **>95%** of Indian rare-disease patients are excluded purely by drug price

> **Real case — Shaurya Singh, age 13.** Hunter Syndrome — a treatable lysosomal disorder that destroys the brain and organs without enzyme replacement. He waited years for treatment that never came. He died in August 2025. A cross-party group of 45 doctor-MPs has reported that other previously-stabilised children are now relapsing as the ₹50-lakh cap exhausts. *Source: Global Health NOW, November 2025.*

**Where Nidaan plugs in:** Surfaces the drug, the manufacturer-supply / compassionate-use pathways (the Genzyme route that kept Nidhi Shirol alive for ten years), the import status, the cost in ₹, and eligible top-up schemes — in the same report as the diagnosis.

### Link 5 — Time (even when the chain holds, time runs out)

Diagnosis is not the finish line. The infusion centre is hours away. The equipment fails. The fund release is delayed by months. Every hour the chain holds, the patient is alive.

- **30%** of children with rare diseases die before age 5
- **69%** of rural lysosomal-storage-disorder patients missed life-sustaining ERT during COVID-19 because they could not make the journey to an infusion centre *(Mumbai tertiary hospital study, PMC)*

> **Real case — Nidhi Shirol, India's first known Pompe patient.** Diagnosed at age 7 after her parents visited 40+ hospitals across India. Samples were sent to Delhi, then to the Netherlands. Treatment access was secured only via a free-supply programme from the manufacturer (Genzyme). She lived a normal childhood for ten years on enzyme replacement therapy. At a college event, the UPS battery on her ventilator failed silently — she suffered a massive cardiac arrest. She died in 2017, aged 24, from a preventable equipment failure. Her father Prasanna co-founded ORDI — India's first rare-disease patient advocacy organisation. *Sources: The Indian Express, SCMP, The Better India.*

**Where Nidaan plugs in:** Compresses Links 1–4 into a single afternoon. Diagnosis at the first visit. Lab requisition emailed. NPRD application drafted. Treatment-centre directions provided. By the time the patient leaves the consulting room, half the work that previously took six years is already in motion.

## The Legal Landscape (Why now matters)

Three court cases bracket this work in time:

- **2014: Mohd Ahmed (Minor) v. Union of India.** A 7-year-old Gaucher patient. The Delhi High Court established that exorbitant cost cannot deny access to life-saving treatment. Foundational right-to-health grounding for rare-disease care.
- **October 2024: Master Arnesh Shaw v. Union of India.** The Delhi High Court ordered ₹974 crore allocated to the National Fund for Rare Diseases for 2024–26, ordered MoHFW to release stuck funds, and held drug manufacturers (Sarepta in this case) accountable for supply delays. The court was unequivocal: *"Just because of the exorbitant price of the drug or treatment, patients, especially children, suffering from a rare disease ought not to be deprived of treatment for their condition."*
- **March 2026: Supreme Court hearing.** The Union government has appealed the Arnesh Shaw judgment. Final hearing scheduled. The legal foundation for rare-disease care is being decided right now.

The legal infrastructure is being forced into existence by litigation. The **clinical infrastructure to act on a favourable ruling does not yet exist.** That is the gap Nidaan is built into.

## How Nidaan Works (The Technical Architecture)

Nidaan is a **multi-agent clinical decision support system** built around a 6-agent hierarchical pipeline. The agents simulate a real teaching-hospital case conference: fast screeners, parallel sub-specialists, and a synthesizer that merges their reports.

### The Pipeline

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  PATIENT submits symptoms (or DOCTOR submits a clinical note)   │
└────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────┘
                             │
                             ▼
              ┌──────────────────────────────┐
              │ Layer 1 · Screeners          │   Claude Haiku 4.5
              │  Common-disease screener     │   (fast triage, ~10s)
              │  + Phenotype extractor (HPO) │
              └──────────────┬───────────────┘
                             │
              ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
              ▼              ▼              ▼
        ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐
        │ Layer 2  │   │ Layer 2  │   │ Layer 2  │  Claude Sonnet 4.6
        │ Metabolic│   │  Neuro-  │   │  Immuno- │  (parallel, ~3 min)
        │  agent   │   │ genetic  │   │  logic   │
        └─────┬────┘   └─────┬────┘   └─────┬────┘
              │              │              │
              └──────────────┼──────────────┘
                             ▼
              ┌──────────────────────────────┐
              │ Layer 3 · Synthesizer        │   Claude Opus 4.7
              │  merges, ranks, surfaces     │   (~1 min)
              │  disagreements, proposes     │
              │  resolving test              │
              └──────────────┬───────────────┘
                             │
                             ▼
              Case Conference Report  (5–7 min total)
              + Treatment Center map
              + NPRD application draft
              + Patient-facing summary in plain language
```

### Why three specialists in parallel, not one big model

A single model summarising a rare-disease case has a systematic failure mode: it averages across specialties and the most-probable explanation dominates. Three independent specialists with different priors will *disagree* — and the disagreement is the most clinically valuable output.

**Disagreement is the feature, not a bug.** When the metabolic specialist says Gaucher (89%) and the neurogenetic specialist flags Niemann-Pick type C (61%), the synthesizer surfaces both and proposes the single test that resolves it: *"Filipin staining on skin fibroblasts — if positive, NPC-C confirmed; if negative, Gaucher differential proceeds."*

This is epistemically honest. It shows the doctor exactly where uncertainty lives and exactly what test removes it.

### The Tools Layer

The agents call live medical knowledge bases via tools (no fabrication, every claim cited):

- **HPO (Human Phenotype Ontology)** — `ontology.jax.org` — free, no key. Maps free-text findings to HP: term IDs.
- **NCBI MedGen** via E-utilities — replaces OMIM. NIH's authoritative clinical-genetics database with cross-referenced OMIM IDs, HPO-linked clinical features, gene associations, inheritance patterns. Free, no key. Covers 8,000+ rare diseases.
- **Wikipedia REST API** — fallback for clinical narrative + infobox extraction (OMIM number, ORPHA code, gene from infoboxes). Free, no key.
- **PubMed E-utilities** — `eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov` — Indian epidemiology, prevalence studies, case series. Free, no key.
- **Orphanet** — when accessible; falls back to Wikipedia infobox extraction otherwise.

All tool responses are cached in-memory (30-min TTL) to deduplicate calls across the three parallel specialists. Every disease in the output must trace to a real tool call. Hallucinated conditions are dropped by the synthesizer.

### Safeguards (Why the tool is trustworthy, not just useful)

| Failure mode | How it's addressed |
|---|---|
| **Zebra hammer / over-diagnosis of rare disease** | Layer 1 includes a dedicated common-disease screener whose sole job is ruling out malaria, TB, kala-azar, nutritional causes *before* the rare-disease specialists activate. If the screener resolves the case, no rare workup is produced. |
| **False reassurance** | Confidence percentages are explicit. Low-confidence differentials (<30%) are flagged as speculative. The report shows what was *not* flagged by each specialist, not just what was. |
| **Hallucination** | Every disease in the output must have an OMIM ID + clinical evidence sourced from a live tool call. Agents producing a disease name without a valid identifier have their claim dropped by the synthesizer. The full tool-call log is visible in the report. |
| **Documentation bias toward Western variants** | The specialist system prompts include explicit Indian epidemiology priors: Gaucher Types 2/3 prevalence, SMA carrier frequency (1 in 38, higher than Western), high-consanguinity priors, and the documented misdiagnosis pathways specific to India (Gaucher → malaria/kala-azar, Wilson's → hepatitis, SMA → nutritional deficiency). |
| **Over-reliance** | The UI states clearly: *"Nidaan surfaces clinical patterns and escalation signals. It does not replace physician judgment."* No diagnosis is confirmed without explicit doctor action. |

## The Platform: Six Stakeholders, One Ecosystem

The diagnostic pipeline is Layer 1. Every other layer is downstream from clinical usage — no separate data collection.

### Doctor (GP / Specialist)
The 6-agent pipeline. Any first-line doctor with internet access. Free-text input, structured output in 5–7 minutes. Confirmation, patient invite, specialist consult request, NPRD application draft — all from the case detail page.

### Patient
Plain-language health record. Sees their diagnosis explained without HPO jargon, the treatment pathway, the nearest CoE on a map, the government schemes they qualify for, and a real-time status thread of their case. Owns the record across providers.

### Specialist
Inbound consultation requests with the full case packet — patient text + AI report + agreement/disagreement analysis + GP's specific question. Reviews asynchronously. One specialist multiplies their reach 20-fold without leaving their desk.

### Researcher
Three-level consent-gated cohort access. Researcher queries: *"How many confirmed GBA-Gaucher patients in Maharashtra consented to research contact?"* — gets a count. Never identifying records. Minimum-cohort floor (n ≥ 3) for privacy. Formal data requests routed through admin approval.

### Government (MoHFW)
Aggregate epidemiology dashboard. State × disease × quarter. Diagnostic delay trends. Treatment access gaps. Centre-of-Excellence coverage maps. CSV export for policy work. Anonymised by construction — no individual records ever surface.

### Admin
User management, research request approvals, case auditing. Gates the system without owning patient data.

All six roles route through `/auth/login`. No mock-session backdoors. Each role can only see what its layout permits — a researcher trying to open `/doctor/cases` is redirected to login.

## The Care Pathway: From Recognition to Treatment

Diagnosis is not the finish line. It is the starting gun.

For Arohi's family, diagnosis came too late and government funding never arrived. For Nidhi Shirol, diagnosis came only after 40 hospitals — and the equipment that eventually killed her was never systematically tracked. For the 12-year-old in rural Maharashtra, diagnosis came at autopsy.

In every case, the failure was not just that the disease was not recognised. **Once recognised, there was no bridge to treatment.** The family was handed a name and left alone to navigate government schemes, lab bookings, specialist queues, and financing — in a system not designed to be navigated.

Nidaan treats the care pathway as a product problem, not a patient problem.

### What happens immediately after the doctor confirms the diagnosis

**1. NPRD application drafted.** Pre-filled from case data: patient demographics, OMIM ID, gene, confirmed diagnosis, CoE that confirmed it, doctor's certification. Doctor reviews and submits with one click.

**2. Centre of Excellence routing.** Geographic distance + specialty + capacity check. Patient sees: *"FRIGE Institute, Ahmedabad — 12 km. Enzyme assay confirmed available. Telemedicine referral: 48-hour response window."*

**3. Drug-pricing transparency.** What the drug costs. What NPRD covers. What top-up schemes apply (PM-JAY, state-level, ICMR registry). Manufacturer compassionate-use programmes (Genzyme/Sanofi for Pompe, Takeda for Gaucher).

**4. Patient-owned record.** Diagnosis explained in plain language. Treatment pathway with timelines. Care timeline updated automatically as events happen. Travels with the patient across providers.

**5. Three-level research consent.** Patient controls what data leaves the platform.
   - *Level 1 (default):* anonymised epidemiology only — your case adds to the state-level rare-disease map
   - *Level 2 (opt-in):* anonymised clinical data shared with ICMR registry / FRIGE for genotype-phenotype research on Indian variants
   - *Level 3 (doubly opt-in):* IRB-approved researchers may contact the doctor about study participation; the doctor then asks the patient

This pattern — **Gaucher disease concealed by malaria in an Indian patient** — is documented in the published literature *(PMC10807272)*. It is not fictional. Gujarat accounts for **43%** of Indian Gaucher cases. The N370S GBA variant is the dominant pathogenic variant in Gujarati populations.

## Indian Epidemiology Priors (Baked Into Every Specialist)

| Category | Share of Indian rare-disease burden | Key diseases |
|---|---|---|
| Neuromuscular / neurodevelopmental | 48.9% | DMD (32.9%), trinucleotide repeats, SMA (15.9%) |
| Inborn errors of metabolism | 15.4% | Gaucher (#1 IEM, 11.2% of IEM), other LSDs |
| Primary immunodeficiencies | ~8% | Combined immunodeficiencies, complement deficiencies |

- **SMA carrier frequency in India: 1 in 38** — higher than Western populations *(Sage Journals 2025)*
- **Gaucher Types 2 and 3 (neuronopathic)** are proportionally more prevalent in India and East Asia than the West. Western India accounts for **43%** of Indian Gaucher cases *(FRIGE 100-patient molecular study)*
- **Consanguinity rate:** 4.5% nationally, up to 20–30% in specific endogamous communities. India has **4,600 endogamous population groups.** This raises autosomal recessive priors substantially.

These priors are explicit in the Metabolic and Neurogenetic specialist system prompts.

## Why This Works

**Three structural insights drive the design:**

1. **The bottleneck is recognition, not testing.** The enzyme assay costs ₹2,500. It exists. It only gets ordered if someone first thinks: *this is Gaucher.* Nidaan moves pattern recognition from "the geneticist at AIIMS" to "the GP in Nashik" — without putting a geneticist in every district hospital.

2. **Disagreement is more valuable than consensus.** A single LLM's ranked list flattens uncertainty. Three specialists in parallel preserve disagreement. The synthesizer surfaces it and proposes the test that resolves it. *That* is how a real case conference works.

3. **Diagnosis without care-pathway integration is performance art.** A doctor who confirms a Gaucher diagnosis but cannot file the NPRD application, locate the nearest CoE, or explain compassionate-use options to the family has not done the work. Nidaan's output is the diagnosis *and* the next four moves.

## Visual Identity

Warm-light theme — a deliberate departure from the cold/dark default of clinical software. Cream paper backgrounds, deep ink-navy text, rust accents on critical states (patient inbox, problem indicators), sage on success states (confirmed, synthesizer outputs, "Where Nidaan plugs in"), warm ochre for warnings.

Typography: **Instrument Serif** for editorial headlines (the case-conference report has the gravitas of a published case study); **Roboto Mono** for body text and clinical observations (the look of a typed chart note); **JetBrains Mono** for technical tokens (OMIM IDs, gene symbols, case IDs).

The aesthetic is the topology diagram in the marketing material: a digital hospital board, drawn on paper.
